Efficient ML & AI Infrastructure

To me, efficiency means keeping the information that matters and dropping the work that doesn't. My work in this area began with compressing language models and has moved toward the systems that train agents. In the agent era, I think the unit of efficiency is no longer a single forward pass but a whole trajectory, and the infrastructure that produces agentic data becomes as important as the model itself.

By Chaofan Tao (陶超凡) · Last updated November 9, 2026

Most of my efficiency work began the same way: an existing method failed, or wasted resources, and the useful question was why. Usually the answer was an assumption that had quietly stopped being true. That the tokenizer can be fixed before scaling. That every part of a network tolerates compression equally. That every cached token matters. That waiting for synchronization is free. That GPUs don't fail. That the strongest teacher teaches best. Once the broken assumption is clear, the fix is often simple.

This page follows those questions through a model's life, from design to compression, memory, serving and transfer, and then to the infrastructure for training agents. The last part is where I think the most important efficiency problems now are.

1. Representations and compression

My work in this direction began with quantizing generative language models, which received an ACL 2022 Outstanding Paper Award. Methods that worked for BERT-style encoders fell apart on GPT-style generators, and it wasn't clear why. We traced it to two problems. At low precision, word embeddings collapse until they look almost the same, so a model choosing its next token can no longer tell the options apart. And weights in different modules are spread very differently, so no single quantizer setting suits them all. Token-level contrastive distillation keeps each token's representation distinct, and module-wise dynamic scaling gives each module its own range. The diagnosis mattered more than the method: it explained why generation is harder to compress than classification.

Structured pruning for generative models applied the same idea to removal, learning which components can go. For vision-language models, where images and text carry information very differently, UPop searches for a smaller network across both modalities at once, so the model learns how much to prune from each, and CrossGET merges redundant tokens at run time, using one modality to guide matching in the other.

The vocabulary is a less obvious place to look. Scaling laws usually leave it out, which is why models of similar size ship with very different vocabularies. In Scaling Laws with Vocabulary (NeurIPS), we treated vocabulary size as a scaling dimension in its own right. Three independent ways of estimating the best size agreed: it grows with compute, though more slowly than the rest of the model, and most models under-invest in it. The reason is a trade-off. A bigger vocabulary represents the same text with fewer tokens, but under a limited budget, rare tokens never get trained well. It gives a principled basis for the move toward larger vocabularies in recent models, and an interactive calculator turns it into a practical choice.

2. Memory and inference

In long-context inference, the KV cache soon takes most of the memory, and trimming it evenly wastes it, because layers behave differently. D2O gives each layer its own budget based on how focused its attention is, and keeps a way to recall or merge dropped tokens that resemble kept ones, without any fine-tuning. UNComp looks at the same question through information content, using matrix entropy to find what can be compressed; the same analysis points to the heads and layers responsible for retrieval. Prefilling-dLLM handles diffusion language models, which re-read the whole prompt at every denoising step, by caching the prompt once in chunks and decoding over only the relevant ones. A small detail mattered there: a beginning-of-sequence token at the start of each chunk gives attention a stable anchor, and with it the sparse version can even beat full attention.

Test-time scaling improves answers by spending more compute while generating, often with a small model drafting and a large one checking. In ATTS, when we measured where the time actually went, the main cost was waiting for synchronization, not arithmetic. ATTS replaces those synchronous checks with asynchronous inference and uses conformal prediction, calibrated on the fly, to keep statistical control over how often drafts are rejected.

3. Transfer: matching the teacher to the student

Distillation passes a teacher's ability to a smaller student. A common story says forward KL divergence spreads out to cover the teacher's distribution while reverse KL locks onto its main mode. For LLMs, we found that neither really happens: the two share the same optimum, and the practical difference appears early in training, when forward KL focuses on the most likely tokens and reverse KL on the tail. Adaptive KL weights them accordingly. PerSyn starts from the observation that stronger models are not always better teachers, and sends each prompt to the teacher a particular student learns from best, before any data is generated. The same lesson, that what transfers depends on what the student can absorb, appears again in our agent trajectory work.

4. Systems and reliability

Serving rests on another hidden assumption: that GPUs don't fail. With tensor parallelism across several GPUs, losing one can take the whole service down. AnchorTP keeps the weights and KV caches alive in a separate process that survives the crash, re-splits the model across the remaining GPUs, and plans the least data movement needed to recover.

Training agents brings both lessons, waiting and lost state, into sharper focus. In Lego-RL, almost all of a training attempt is the agent working in a sandbox, and attempts vary widely in length, so synchronous batches keep waiting for the slowest member. Asynchronous scheduling fills those gaps. When weights update mid-attempt, partial rollout pauses generation, loads the new weights and continues from the cached prefix, so long attempts aren't thrown away. Sandbox start-up is dominated by building images, so prebuilt task images and lazy image pulls remove most of that wait, while the grading tools are deliberately baked into every image, because a reward you can reproduce is worth more than one you get slightly faster. Dashboards that break down why each attempt ended let us tell infrastructure failures from genuine policy problems before misreading a reward curve. Single-rollout optimization attacks a different cost: it replaces repeated sampling of the same task with a learned critic, so far fewer trajectories are needed to train.

What we learned

  1. Find the broken assumption before you optimize. Every result here started with asking why an existing method failed. Once the cause was clear, the fix was usually small.
  2. Unevenness is the rule. Layers, modules, modalities, tokens and teachers all differ, and giving each its own budget keeps paying off.
  3. Keep a way back. Dropped tokens that can be recalled, state that survives a crash, attempts that survive a weight update: these make aggressive optimization safe.
  4. At scale, waiting is the real cost. In test-time scaling, serving and agent training alike, the largest gains came from removing waiting rather than from faster arithmetic.
  5. Efficiency decides what a model can learn. Rare tokens that never get enough compute and teacher outputs a student can't absorb show that efficiency is about capability, not only cost.

Where I think this is going

Infrastructure for large-scale agentic data is the new bottleneck

For language models, data meant text. For agents, data means executable environments with a way to check the outcome, and producing them at scale is mostly an infrastructure problem. Building LegoFlow and Terminal-Lego taught us where the cost goes: finding candidate tasks is cheap, but building their environments, proving each one with tests, rolling out trajectories and screening them for leakage and shallow reasoning is where almost all the effort lies, and only a small fraction of candidates survive. I think the scaling unit for agentic data is the verified, reproducible environment, and infrastructure should be judged by how many of those it can build, verify and keep honest per unit of compute. That means fast environment construction and caching, isolated and network-controlled sandboxes, provenance for every task and trajectory, and pipelines designed so that agents themselves can operate them, as LegoFlow's blocks already allow.

Software engineering is the easiest case, because tests give a clean signal. The next wave of agentic data will come from collaborative office work, what I think of as cowork data: documents, spreadsheets, email and multi-application workflows, often with a human in the loop. There, checking the outcome is much harder. It needs simulated applications whose state can be inspected, simulated users who respond realistically, and checkers that judge whether a task was done well rather than merely done. I expect the teams that build this infrastructure, rather than the ones with the most raw logs, to have the best agentic data.

Efficiency should be measured per trajectory, not per call

Most efficiency methods, including many of ours, are evaluated one response at a time. For an agent, a compression or caching choice that loses one detail can cost several extra steps later, and the savings vanish. I think efficiency for agents has to be measured at the level of the whole task: total compute, memory and time to a verified result.

This also changes what memory should keep. An agent's context is dominated by tool output rather than prose, so the idea behind D2O and UNComp, keeping what later steps will need and keeping a way back for what was dropped, should be applied to observations and actions, not just tokens.

In AI-for-AI loops, compute should flow to verification

When AI helps improve AI, each round spends compute on proposing candidates, running them, checking them and confirming the winners. Our agent work suggests that checking is where reliability is won or lost. So I think the efficiency question for these loops is less "how fast can we generate candidates?" and more "how cheaply can we trust a result?" Cheaper reviewers and verifiers, of the kind SWE-Review and SWE-Lego's verifier point to, are likely to be the biggest lever. Progress should also be counted at the same total budget across rounds, including failed attempts and replication, so that an improvement in the loop is not confused with simply spending more.

Long runs need the reliability we expect from serving

Agent training runs and autonomous experiments last for days and touch thousands of sandboxes. The ideas that made serving resilient, separating state from the processes that can crash and recovering with minimal movement, need to become standard for these long runs too, along with records precise enough to resume an experiment and trust its result after a failure.

Open questions

  • What does a verified, reproducible environment for collaborative office work look like, and how do we check outcomes that have no unit tests?
  • How should vocabulary and caches be designed for agents whose context is mostly tool output?
  • How much of agent RL's cost can be removed by better scheduling and fewer rollouts before it starts to cost capability?
  • How should an AI-for-AI loop split its budget between exploring and verifying?

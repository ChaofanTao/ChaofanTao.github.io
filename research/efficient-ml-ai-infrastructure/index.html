Efficient ML & AI Infrastructure

To me, efficiency means keeping the information that matters and dropping the work that doesn't. My work in this area began with compressing language models and has moved toward the systems that train agents. In the agent era, I think the unit of efficiency is no longer a single forward pass but a whole trajectory, and the infrastructure that produces agentic data becomes as important as the model itself.

By Chaofan Tao (陶超凡) · Last updated November 9, 2026

Most of my efficiency work began the same way: an existing method failed, or wasted resources, and the useful question was why. Usually the answer was an assumption that had quietly stopped being true. That the tokenizer can be fixed before scaling. That every part of a network tolerates compression equally. That every cached token matters. That waiting for synchronization is free. That GPUs don't fail. That the strongest teacher teaches best. Once the broken assumption is clear, the fix is often simple.

This page follows those questions through a model's life, from design to compression, memory, serving and transfer, and then to the infrastructure for training agents. The last part is where I think the most important efficiency problems now are.

1. Representations and compression

Generation turned out to be harder to compress than classification, and the reason was in the representations. My work in this direction began with quantizing generative language models, which received an ACL 2022 Outstanding Paper Award. Methods that worked for BERT-style encoders fell apart on GPT-style generators, and it wasn't clear why. We traced it to two problems. At low precision, word embeddings collapse until they look almost the same, so a model choosing its next token can no longer tell the options apart. And weights in different modules are spread very differently, so no single quantizer setting suits them all. Token-level contrastive distillation keeps each token's representation distinct, and module-wise dynamic scaling gives each module its own range.

Structured pruning for generative models applied the same idea to removal, learning which components can go. For vision-language models, where images and text carry information very differently, UPop searches for a smaller network across both modalities at once, so the model learns how much to prune from each, and CrossGET merges redundant tokens at run time, using one modality to guide matching in the other.

The vocabulary is a less obvious place to look. Scaling laws usually leave it out, which is why models of similar size ship with very different vocabularies. In Scaling Laws with Vocabulary, we treated vocabulary size as a scaling dimension in its own right. Three independent ways of estimating the best size agreed: it grows with compute, though more slowly than the rest of the model, and most models under-invest in it. The reason is a trade-off. A bigger vocabulary represents the same text with fewer tokens, but under a limited budget, rare tokens never get trained well. It gives a principled basis for the move toward larger vocabularies in recent models, and an interactive calculator turns it into a practical choice.

2. Memory and inference

In long-context inference, the KV cache soon takes most of the memory, and trimming it evenly wastes it, because layers behave differently. D2O gives each layer its own budget based on how focused its attention is, and keeps a way to recall or merge dropped tokens that resemble kept ones, without any fine-tuning. UNComp looks at the same question through information content, using matrix entropy to find what can be compressed; the same analysis points to the heads and layers responsible for retrieval.

Prefilling-dLLM handles diffusion language models, which re-read the whole prompt at every denoising step, by caching the prompt once in chunks and decoding over only the relevant ones. A small detail mattered there: a beginning-of-sequence token at the start of each chunk gives attention a stable anchor, and with it the sparse version can even beat full attention.

Test-time scaling improves answers by spending more compute while generating, often with a small model drafting and a large one checking. In ATTS, when we measured where the time actually went, the main cost was waiting for synchronization, not arithmetic. ATTS replaces those synchronous checks with asynchronous inference and uses conformal prediction, calibrated on the fly, to keep statistical control over how often drafts are rejected.

3. Transfer: matching the teacher to the student

Distillation passes a teacher's ability to a smaller student. A common story says forward KL divergence spreads out to cover the teacher's distribution while reverse KL locks onto its main mode. For LLMs, we found that neither really happens: the two share the same optimum, and the practical difference appears early in training, when forward KL focuses on the most likely tokens and reverse KL on the tail. Adaptive KL weights them accordingly. PerSyn starts from the observation that stronger models are not always better teachers, and sends each prompt to the teacher a particular student learns from best, before any data is generated. The same lesson, that what transfers depends on what the student can absorb, appears again in our agent trajectory work.

4. Systems and reliability

Serving rests on another hidden assumption: that GPUs don't fail. With tensor parallelism across several GPUs, losing one can take the whole service down. AnchorTP keeps the weights and KV caches alive in a separate process that survives the crash, re-splits the model across the remaining GPUs, and plans the least data movement needed to recover.

Training agents brings both lessons, waiting and lost state, into sharper focus. In Lego-RL, almost all of a training attempt is the agent working in a sandbox, and attempts vary widely in length, so synchronous batches keep waiting for the slowest member; asynchronous scheduling fills those gaps, and partial rollout lets a long attempt survive a weight update by continuing from its cached prefix. Prebuilt task images and lazy image pulls remove most of the sandbox start-up wait, while the grading tools are deliberately baked into every image, because a reward you can reproduce is worth more than one you get slightly faster. How Lego-RL keeps training faithful to what the agent actually did, and how it tells infrastructure failures from policy problems, is covered on the agentic intelligence page.

What we learned

  1. Find the broken assumption before you optimize. Every result here started with asking why an existing method failed. Once the cause was clear, the fix was usually small.
  2. Unevenness is the rule. Layers, modules, modalities, tokens and teachers all differ, and giving each its own budget keeps paying off.
  3. Keep a way back. Dropped tokens that can be recalled, state that survives a crash, attempts that survive a weight update: these make aggressive optimization safe.
  4. At scale, waiting is the real cost. In test-time scaling, serving and agent training alike, the largest gains came from removing waiting rather than from faster arithmetic.
  5. Efficiency decides what a model can learn. Rare tokens that never get enough compute and teacher outputs a student can't absorb show that efficiency is about capability, not only cost.

Some thoughts on the future

These are personal views, each an extension of something the work above taught us.

Infrastructure for large-scale agentic data is the new bottleneck

For agents, data means executable environments with a way to check the outcome. Building LegoFlow and Terminal-Lego showed us where the cost goes: finding candidates is cheap, while building environments, proving tasks with tests, and rolling out and screening trajectories take almost all the effort, and few candidates survive. I think the scaling unit for agentic data is the verified, reproducible environment, and infrastructure should be judged by how many it can build and keep honest per unit of compute.

Software is the easy case, because tests give a clean signal. The next wave is cowork data, documents, spreadsheets, email and multi-app workflows with people in the loop, where checking outcomes is much harder. I expect the teams that build this infrastructure, not those with the most raw logs, to have the best agentic data.

Coding data Cowork data
Environment A repository snapshot in a container Documents, spreadsheets, email, several apps, often a person
Outcome check Original fails the tests, the fix passes App state plus a quality judgment; rarely unit tests
Main difficulty Answer leakage, scale, shallow trajectories Simulating apps and people; judging "done well"
Infrastructure Fast builds, isolated sandboxes, anti-leakage, provenance Simulated apps and users, quality checkers, replayable sessions

Efficiency should be measured per trajectory, not per call

Most efficiency methods, including ours, are judged one response at a time, but for an agent, dropping one detail can cost several extra steps later. Efficiency should be measured per task, as total compute, memory and time to a verified result, and the D2O and UNComp idea of keeping a way back should apply to observations and actions, not only tokens.

Very long-horizon runs need layered memory and resumable state

No context window holds weeks of tool output, so memory will need layers: a short working context, compressed summaries, and a searchable store for exact details, a project-level version of D2O's recall. Since machines and sandboxes will fail over weeks, AnchorTP's separation of state from crashing processes and partial rollout's resuming should become standard.

AI for AI and AI for Science: compute should flow to verification

In AI-for-AI loops, checking is where reliability is won or lost, so cheaper reviewers and verifiers, of the kind SWE-Review points to, are likely the biggest lever. Science sharpens this, because the expensive step is the experiment. The draft-and-check pattern from ATTS fits: a cheap surrogate screens many candidates, the expensive experiment checks the most promising, and calibrated rules set how much to filter, at the risk of removing exactly the surprising ones. Failed experiments should be kept, with their settings.

Open questions

Questions I find most important for this direction, some raised by our own work and some by where the field is now.

  • Is there a scaling law for agentic data? For text we know how loss scales with tokens. For agents the unit is a verified environment, and we do not know how capability scales with their number, variety and difficulty, what each should cost, or how to check environments that have no unit tests, such as cowork tasks.
  • What is compute-optimal for an agent? We know how to split training compute between parameters and data, and that test-time compute can beat a larger model. For an agent, a fixed budget can go to a bigger model, more turns, more parallel attempts or more verification, and there is no equivalent rule for the trajectory.
  • How far can training and inference drift apart before RL breaks? Asynchronous and partial rollouts make data slightly off-policy, and even the same weights can produce different numbers in an inference engine and a trainer. Throughput rewards more drift and stability punishes it; the safe boundary is still found by trial and error.
  • Can small models learn long-horizon agent skills? The best teacher is the one a student can learn from, but it is unclear whether a small model can absorb planning and self-checking from traces, or whether some of that only appears at scale.
  • Which efficiency techniques survive new architectures? Diffusion language models, mixtures of experts and hybrid attention each break assumptions behind KV caching, quantization and speculative decoding.
